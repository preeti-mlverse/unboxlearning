Why we're building UnboxEd

October 2026 · 4 min read

There has never been more educational content. Textbooks are online, lectures are recorded, every company has a wiki and a shelf of slide decks. And yet most learners still meet that knowledge the same way: a long page, a long video, a quiz at the end.

That format treats exposure as learning. It assumes everyone reads the same language equally well, starts from the same place and needs the same explanation. None of that is true.

Reading isn't learning

Decades of learning research point the same way. People remember far more when they retrieve an idea than when they re-read it. Ideas last longer when they come back at spaced intervals. Pictures and words together beat either alone. And people understand cause and effect best when they can change the cause and watch the effect.

Good teachers and good courses already do these things. The problem is cost: designing a single hour of interactive learning takes an expert many hours. So most knowledge never gets that treatment. It stays a PDF.

Unboxing the knowledge

UnboxEd starts from material people already trust: a chapter, a policy, a lecture, an expert's explanation. It works out what kind of knowledge is inside, which ideas matter and what comes before what. Then it designs the learning: a visual explanation for one idea, an ordering game for a process, a short scenario for a judgement call, a review a few days later for a term that needs to stick.

The person who teaches stays in charge. They choose the learners, the goal and the language, approve the outline, and can edit every card before anyone sees it. Every card is checked against the source it came from.

Language, voice and reach

Much of the world's best learning material exists in English, while many learners think in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali or another language. Translation helps, but understanding needs the explanation to change too: examples that make sense locally, words pitched at the learner's level, and a tutor they can ask in their own language, by voice if reading is hard.

That is why we're designing UnboxEd to be multilingual, voice-first and mobile-first from the start, rather than adding those things later.

What we'll measure

We don't want to count clicks. As we pilot UnboxEd with educators, schools and organizations, we'll be looking at whether more people can reach the learning, whether concepts become clearer, whether learning lasts, and whether educators can create more with less effort.
